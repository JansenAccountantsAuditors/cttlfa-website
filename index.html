Referees & Match Officials

The referee is central to every game. This page explains the role of the referee, how our refereeing structure works, where it sits within our Constitution, SAFA and the FIFA Laws of the Game, what good officiating looks like, and how to become a match official with the association.

Our match officials make the game happen every weekend. If you have ever thought about taking up the whistle, the association will help you get started.
Wayne Engelbrecht, Head of Referees

The role of the referee

The referee is the sole on-field authority for a match. He or she applies the seventeen Laws of the Game, keeps players safe, manages the contest fairly, and protects the spirit of the game. The referee's decisions on points of fact connected with play are final. Assistant referees support the referee in the senior promotion leagues.

Good refereeing is quiet, consistent and respected. It lets the football, and the players, be the story. Every club, player, coach and spectator shares a duty to respect match officials and to support them in doing a difficult job well.

How refereeing is structured at CTTLFA

Head of Referees

An elected member of the Management Committee who oversees the operational work of the Referees Committee, reports monthly to Mancom, and is the liaison with SAFA Cape Town and SAFA National on courses and match-official matters (Constitution 7.14).

Referees Committee

Runs refereeing day to day: it registers and grades officials, tables the referee lists each season, appoints referees to the senior promotion leagues through its appointment convenor, and maintains the referees' code of conduct.

Cape Town Tygerberg Referees

The collective of match officials: registered CTT referees, non-club affiliated referees and the club referees supplied by every affiliated club. Together they cover the league and cup programme each weekend.

The SAFA & CAF course pathway

Beyond match-day officiating, football's coaching and development qualifications follow the SAFA and CAF course pathway, delivered in our region by SAFA Cape Town. It runs from the entry-level D course up to the elite A course, with each level building on the one before. Match officials, in turn, progress through SAFA's officiating grades from local level into regional, national and ultimately FIFA appointments. Both pathways start with a course through SAFA Cape Town.

No card, no play

Every player must be registered for the season, and all coaches, managers and referees must be SAFA (Cape Town) card-carrying members.

Everyone plays

Unlimited rolling substitutions. In the Under-7 to Under-9 game every child must play at least one full period.

No fixed goalkeepers

Before Under-10 a different child goes in goal each period, so no one is pigeon-holed as the keeper too early.

No offside, no camping

There is no offside, but players may not "camp" more than two metres beyond the last defender. Officials guide the children.

Only the referee on the pitch

Coaches and substitutes stay off the field. Where space allows they may stand behind the goal near the corner, never behind the posts.

Safety first

The hosting club has a trained first aider on match days. No spectators behind the goal line, and everyone stays two metres off the touchline.

What our Constitution says

Development is a founding object

Object 2.1 of the Constitution commits the association to the encouragement, promotion, development and administration of football across its area, on a basis of non-racism, non-sexism and non-ageism, so that age is never a basis for excluding any child from the game. Mini football is where that development begins.

Youth football has its own leadership

The Constitution creates dedicated Youth Football and Technical Development portfolios, chaired by the Vice President: Youth Football (article 7.10), who is responsible for the youth and development leagues, for setting up age-group structures and competitions with the Operations Manager, and for keeping youth on the agenda at every General Meeting.

In short: mini and youth football are not an afterthought in our rules. They sit in the objects of the association and carry their own committee and office-bearer, because the youngest players are where the association's future is built.

Safeguarding in Our Football

Every child and vulnerable adult who plays under the Cape Town Tygerberg LFA has the right to enjoy the game in a safe place. This page explains what safeguarding means, the everyday good practice that keeps sport safe, how a club can start building a safe environment, and how to raise a concern. Most clubs are at the beginning of this journey, and that is exactly where to start.

A Cape Town Tygerberg junior football initiative

Why safeguarding matters

Safeguarding is protecting children and adults at risk from abuse, harm and neglect, and giving them a positive, enjoyable experience of football. It is not an add-on. It is a shared duty that sits with every club, coach, official, volunteer, parent and spectator in the association.

Clubs have a duty of care towards the young people in their charge. Getting safeguarding right protects children first, and it also protects volunteers and the club by making expectations clear and by dealing with concerns properly and consistently.

Start where you are. We know most clubs do not yet have a formal safeguarding process in place, and that is understood. The aim is not to have everything sorted overnight. It is to begin: to talk about it, name someone to hold it, and take the first few steps. The association is putting a simple safeguarding policy in place, aligned to the SAFA National Safeguarding Policy, that clubs can adopt, so no club has to start from a blank page.

Creating safe spaces: everyday good practice

Most of safeguarding is simply good coaching and a good club culture. These are the everyday habits that keep children safe and make the game more enjoyable for everyone. Many of them cost little or nothing, and every club can start today.

An open environment

Coach and work with children in the open, where others can see. Avoid being alone and out of sight with a child.

Appropriate distance

Keep physical contact appropriate and necessary. When you need to guide a young player, explain what you are doing and why.

Positive role models

Set the tone through your own conduct. Children learn respect, fair play and self-control from the adults around them.

Constructive feedback

Encourage and correct without shouting down or belittling. Feedback should build a player up, never tear them down.

Awareness

Know the signs, know who your Safeguarding Officer is, and know that a concern should always be passed on.

Skill and development

Well-run, age-appropriate sessions where children are learning and enjoying themselves are safer sessions.

The six safeguarding principles

Empowerment

People are supported to make their own decisions and give informed consent. Children are listened to and taken seriously.

Prevention

It is better to act before harm occurs. Good practice, awareness and clear boundaries stop problems before they start.

Proportionality

The response is the least intrusive one appropriate to the risk presented, and is measured and fair.

Protection

Support and representation are given to those in greatest need, and action is taken to keep them safe.

Partnership

Safeguarding works best when clubs, families, the association and local services work together.

Accountability

Everyone is clear about their role, and safeguarding practice is transparent and open to scrutiny.

Building a safe club, step by step

You do not need all of this on day one. Think of it as a path to build along, starting with the first two or three and adding the rest over time. This is what a well-safeguarded club looks like once it is fully in place.

1
A Safeguarding Officer

A named, contactable person responsible for safeguarding at the club, who receives concerns, keeps records securely and knows how to escalate.

2
Vetting of adults

Anyone working with children is screened before they start: a SAPS police clearance, the National Register for Sex Offenders under the Criminal Law (Sexual Offences and Related Matters) Amendment Act 32 of 2007, and Part B of the National Child Protection Register under the Children's Act 38 of 2005.

3
A safeguarding policy

A written policy that says how the club keeps children safe, who is responsible, and what to do when a concern is raised. Reviewed regularly and shared with everyone.

4
A code of conduct

Clear standards of behaviour for coaches, volunteers, players, parents and spectators, agreed up front and applied consistently.

5
Training and awareness

Everyone who works with young people knows the basics: what abuse looks like, what good practice looks like, and how to report a concern.

6
Risk assessment

Activities, trips, tournaments and travel are thought through in advance so that foreseeable risks to children are managed.

Not sure where to begin? Start with three things: name a person at the club who will hold safeguarding, agree a short code of conduct for coaches, players and parents, and adopt the association's safeguarding policy once it is available. That is a real start, and you can build from there.

Recognising when something is wrong

Categories of abuse

1Emotional — persistent lack of love or attention, harsh criticism, threats, or being constantly put down.
2Physical — being hurt on purpose, including through excessive or inappropriate training or exercise.
3Sexual — any sexual activity or contact with a child, in person or online.
4Neglect — failing to meet a child's basic needs, or leaving them at risk of harm, cold, hunger or injury.

Poor practice to watch for

Unnecessary physical contact, spending time alone with a child away from others, favouritism, sharing lifts or private messages inappropriately, or ignoring the club's own rules. Poor practice is not always abuse, but it creates risk and must be addressed. Trust your instincts. If something does not feel right, raise it.

If a child tells you something

Do

  • Stay calm, listen carefully and take them seriously.
  • Reassure them they were right to tell you.
  • Record what was said, in the child's own words, as soon as you can.
  • Report it to the Safeguarding Officer promptly.

Don't

  • Promise to keep it a secret. You cannot.
  • Investigate, question or interview the child yourself.
  • Confront the person the concern is about.
  • Delay. Pass it on the same day.

Reporting a concern, step by step

1
Something worries you

A child tells you something, or you notice signs of harm or poor practice.

2
Respond well

Stay calm, listen and reassure. Do not promise to keep it secret, and do not investigate.

3
Write it down

Record what was said or seen, in the child's own words, with the date and time.

4
Report the same day

Tell your club's Safeguarding Officer without delay.

5
The Officer acts

They assess, keep a secure record, and refer the matter to the authorities where needed.

If a child is in immediate danger, contact the SAPS on 10111 or Childline South Africa on 116 first, then tell the Safeguarding Officer.

Clubs Seeking Affiliation

Thinking of bringing your club into Cape Town Tygerberg football? This page sets out who can apply, how the process works, the deadlines that matter, and what it costs, all drawn from the association's Constitution.

Affiliation is decided once a year, by the members. New clubs are admitted at the Annual General Meeting, held in November. The members vote on each application. The Management Committee considers applications first, but it is the AGM that admits a club (Constitution Articles 4.1 and 6.2).

Who can apply

Full membership

Open to a club made up of at least two senior and two junior teams. Full members have a vote and take part in the league and cup programme (Article 4.1.4).

Associate club

For clubs that do not yet meet the two-senior, two-junior test: youth only, seniors only, ladies only, or a mix. If the club later grows to two-plus-two, it applies afresh for full membership (Article 4.5).

Organisations

Bodies that provide football but do not match the definition of a club may affiliate as organisations, and fall under the association's articles, rules and disciplinary code (Article 4.2.2).

How affiliation works

1
Apply in writing, at least 45 days before the AGM

Your written application must reach the association no later than forty-five days before the Annual General Meeting, and must include your club's own constitution (Articles 4.1.1 and 4.1.2).

2
The Management Committee considers it

Applications are considered by the Management Committee ahead of the AGM. The committee prepares the matter for the members to decide.

3
The members vote at the AGM

The Annual General Meeting, held in November, admits new clubs. Admission is a decision of the members, not of the committee alone (Articles 4.1.5 and 6.2).

4
Complete the form and pay the fees

On admission, complete the affiliation form and settle the entrance, affiliation and administration fees to activate membership for the season (Article 4.2.3.1).

5
One-year probation

New members serve a one-year probationary period. Their first season is assessed at the following AGM before full membership is granted (Article 4.1.7).

Step-by-step for clubs

Lodge a protest
  1. On the day — your captain or a listed team official records the protest with the referee before or during the match, and it is noted and signed on both team returns.
  2. Within 4 days of the match — submit a written intention to protest on the prescribed form, signed by a club executive, with the R700 fee and proof of payment (showing club, team, opponent and date). No fee in time, no protest.
  3. Within the next 2 days — submit your full written grounds with supporting statements, documents and evidence.
  4. Attend the hearing — both clubs are asked to be present at the Disciplinary Committee, which decides the protest on its merits.
  5. Not satisfied? Proceed to an appeal within 7 days.
Lodge a complaint
  1. Within 4 days of the incident — lodge a written intention to complain on the prescribed form, signed by a club executive, with the R700 fee. Not against a referee's playing decision unless you allege corruption.
  2. Within the next 2 days — submit the full written complaint with all the facts and the exact rule or article you say was breached.
  3. The Disciplinary Committee decides — outcomes can include a points forfeiture.
  4. Escalate via an appeal if needed.
Respond to a disciplinary charge
  1. Note any automatic suspension — a red card carries an automatic next-match suspension.
  2. Ask for particulars — you may request full particulars of the charge before the hearing, and you are entitled to at least 72 hours' written notice of it.
  3. Prepare — gather evidence and witnesses; you may arrange legal representation, and a minor must be assisted by a parent or guardian.
  4. Confirm attendance — if you cannot attend, tell the Association at least 24 hours before with your reasons, or the hearing may proceed without you.
  5. After the decision — if only the terms are given, you have 10 days to request the written grounds. Pay any fine promptly — a suspension runs until it is paid.
Lodge an appeal
  1. Check it can be appealed — warnings, reprimands, suspensions of three or more matches, and small fines cannot be appealed.
  2. Within 7 days of the decision — send a written notice of intention to appeal to the Appeal Committee, stating whether you appeal the verdict, the sentence, or both, and pay the R1 000 deposit before the deadline.
  3. Within a further 5 days — submit your written grounds: an inaccurate view of the facts and/or a wrong application of the law.
  4. The appeal is heard within 21 days — the Appeal Committee decides the matter on the verdict, the sentence, or both.
  5. Still aggrieved? You may escalate to SAFA Cape Town (R5 000) — but first send a copy of your notice and reasons to the association office (General Secretary).
Where to submit. Protests, complaints and appeals are lodged in writing with the association, addressed to the General Secretary, with the fee and proof of payment. The Information Regulator has compiled a guide, in terms of section 10 of PAIA, on how to use the Act. It is available from the Regulator at inforegulator.org.za or on 010 023 5200.

3. Records held by the association

The association holds records in the following broad categories:

  • Governance: the Constitution, rules and regulations, the disciplinary code, and minutes and resolutions of the association’s structures.
  • Financial: audited annual financial statements, treasurer and management reports, and records kept for accounting, tax and NPO/PBO purposes.
  • Competitions and membership: club affiliation, team entries, fixtures, results and league records.
  • Statutory and correspondence: registration records, and general correspondence with members, SAFA and third parties.

4. Records available without a formal request

Certain records are published for members and the public and do not require a PAIA request, including the Constitution, the rules and regulations and the disciplinary code, and the audited annual financial statements presented to members. Records are also made available where other legislation, such as the Nonprofit Organisations Act 71 of 1997 or the Income Tax Act 58 of 1962, requires it.

5. How to request access to a record

To request a record that is not already published, submit a written request to the Information Officer at ops@cttlfa.com, using the prescribed form (Form 2 of the Regulations under PAIA). Your request should give enough detail to identify the record, the form of access required, your contact details, and the right you are seeking to exercise where the request relates to the protection of a right. The association will respond within the period allowed by PAIA, normally 30 days.

6. Fees

PAIA provides for a request fee and access fees, in the amounts prescribed in the Regulations. Where a fee is payable, the association will notify you and may require payment, or a deposit, before the record is prepared or provided. The current schedule of prescribed fees is available from the office and from the Information Regulator.

7. Grounds on which access may be refused

Access may be refused only on the grounds set out in Chapter 4 of Part 3 of PAIA, for example to protect the privacy of a third party, commercial or confidential information, or records subject to legal privilege. Where part of a record may be refused, access will be given to the remainder where it is reasonably possible to sever it.

8. Remedies

If a request is refused, or is not dealt with as PAIA requires, you may lodge a complaint with the Information Regulator or apply to a competent court. Complaints to the Regulator can be made at eservices.inforegulator.org.za or by email to PAIAComplaints@inforegulator.org.za.

9. Availability of this manual

This manual is available on this website and at the association’s office. A version in the format prescribed by the Information Regulator is available from the Information Officer on request.

History & Heritage

More than thirty years of organised football in the Tygerberg region: the clubs, the competitions and the volunteers who built the association.

Our story

The Cape Town Tygerberg Local Football Association was established in 1993 to organise and grow amateur football in the Tygerberg region. Today its member clubs stretch right across Cape Town, from the northern suburbs to the Atlantic seaboard, the southern peninsula and out to the eastern suburbs. As a Local Football Association affiliated to SAFA Cape Town, and through it to the South African Football Association, the association runs league and cup competitions from the Premier Division down, across senior, reserve, veterans, women's and youth football.

Three decades on, the association is home to dozens of affiliated clubs and hundreds of teams, run by a democratically elected Management Committee and a body of volunteers, coaches and match officials. The heritage of the game in Tygerberg lives in its clubs, many of which have served their communities for generations.
